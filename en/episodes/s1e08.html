Clearing and settlement

The authorisation reserves the funds for about a week. The merchant is not paid yet: the sale still has to be captured, meaning sent into clearing.

5 min · read 4 concepts 4 quiz
Concept 1 / 4 · The second message

Authorise, then capture

The authorisation reserves the funds, but does not pay the merchant. It still has to be captured, meaning sent into clearing. There are three ways to do it:

  • a capture message, sent automatically right after each sale;
  • a second message, grouped at the end of the day for all authorised sales: the batch;
  • depending on the network and the channel, a single message that authorises and captures at once (single message).

When there is a batch, it leaves either from the terminal, automatically or triggered by the merchant, or directly from the server (backend).

Remember: once authorised, the payment is guaranteed. Once captured, it becomes a debt that the cardholder's bank has to settle.

Concept 2 / 4 · The time limits

Two time limits, two starting points

An authorisation has a limited lifespan. It reserves the funds and guarantees payment for about a week, depending on the network. If the capture (or the batch upload) arrives after that, the sale can still be accepted technically. But the authorisation no longer protects it: depending on the network and the state of the cardholder's account, the issuer can dispute it afterwards.

Once the authorisation is captured, another count starts. When a contract promises “D+1”, D is the day of the batch, not the day of the sale. And on weekends and some public holidays, there is no payout.

Contract: payout at D+1 after the batch. The batch leaves every evening at 11 pm. A sale takes place on Thursday at 11:30 pm.

Which day does the merchant receive the money?
Pick one, the answer appears.
D+1 is counted from the batch, and weekends don't count. Thirty minutes late for Thursday's batch, and the payout moves from Friday to Monday.

Concept 3 / 4 · Clearing

Nobody wires €42

The batches from the whole day are pooled. Each bank or institution has just one net position: everything it owes the others, minus everything the others owe it. Millions of sales boil down to one amount per institution, settled once. That is clearing. In France, for CB, it is STET that calculates these positions.

In millions of euros. Thousands of crossed sales, three net movements: 15 + 5 received, 20 paid, the sum is zero.

The merchant lives the same thing at their own scale. Their payout is a balance, from which refunds, chargebacks and, depending on the contract, fees (acquirer, interchange, network) have been taken off. Hence reconciliation, the exercise every accounting department knows: finding each sale behind a payout.

Remember: netting happens at two levels.
Between banks
Each one settles a balance, interchange already deducted (episode 2).
For the merchant
The acquirer pays out their sales minus refunds and chargebacks. Its fee is deducted from the payout or invoiced separately, depending on the contract.

Concept 4 / 4 · The calendar

A European calendar

The balances calculated by STET are settled once a day, in the euro-area central banks' settlement system. Its calendar sets the days on which banks pay each other, and therefore the days on which the acquirer is itself settled.

This calendar is the same across the whole euro area, and it is not the French public-holiday calendar: on 14 July or 11 November, banks settle as normal.

Remember: 1 January, Good Friday, Easter Monday, 1 May, 25 and 26 December: six days without settlement between banks across the whole euro area, on top of weekends.
What really matters
The credit delay has become a commercial line: the trend is towards D+1, which some acquirers charge for as an option and others include. Comparing two offers on the fee alone means forgetting that a day of cash has a price.
